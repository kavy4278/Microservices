# Crumbly: Gaps in the Earning Idea

Analysis of the stakeholder financial benefits document, with fixes and worked examples.

**Memory trick:** ask three questions for every benefit: **Who pays? Who gets? What is left for us?**

The stakeholder document is strong on **who gets** (riders, shops, NGOs, donors). It is weak on **who pays** and **what is left for Crumbly**. The gaps below are listed from biggest to smallest.

## Gap 1: The doc never says how the platform earns

Every benefit in the document is money going **out** or **to users**: stars, rider pay, shop sales. Nowhere does it say what Crumbly keeps.

**Fix:** take a cut on paid orders.

**Worked example (made-up numbers):**

1. An NGO orders 100 meals from a cloud kitchen at ₹60 each, so ₹6,000.
2. Crumbly keeps a **10% commission**, which is **₹600**.
3. The delivery fee is ₹150. The rider gets ₹120 and Crumbly keeps **₹30**.
4. The platform earns **₹630** on one order.

## Gap 2: Stars are a cost, not income

Free food redeemed at shops means someone has to pay the shop. If nobody does, shops will leave.

| Problem | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Who funds redemption? | A shop gives a ₹50 snack for 100 stars and gets no money back |
| Fake listings to farm stars | Someone lists the same food again and again |
| Star inflation | Too many stars are given and the rewards lose value |

**Fix:**

- Have **CSR sponsors or brands fund the reward pool**.
- Let shops treat redemption as **marketing spend** that brings them customers.
- Give stars **only after OTP-verified delivery**, the same rule as the first business model document.

## Gap 3: Who pays the riders?

The first business model made riders mostly volunteers. The stakeholder document says they earn per delivery. Those two do not match, and the money source is not named. Surplus food pickups are also unpredictable, so a rider cannot earn a steady living from them. The "employment" claim is too strong for v1.

**Fix:** charge a small delivery fee to the donor or NGO, or fund it from sponsors. Say "extra income" instead of "employment."

## Gap 4: Paid NGO orders may not work

Most NGOs have little money. Who will actually pay for the ₹6,000 order? Possibly corporate CSR donors or event sponsors, and the document does not mention them.

**Fix:** add a **"sponsor a meal" option**, where a company or individual pays for meals that go to an NGO. This also becomes a revenue stream.

## Gap 5: Two businesses are mixed together

| Surplus rescue | Paid food orders |
| --- | --- |
| Leftover food | Freshly cooked to order |
| Free, urgent | Paid, planned |
| Mission: reduce waste | Mission: sales |

Paid orders are close to Swiggy or Zomato territory. They also make the app drift from its mission, which the guardrails in the first document warn about.

**Fix:** keep the two flows separate in the app, and label paid orders as **"Food Drive Orders."**

## Gap 6: It conflicts with the first business model

- First document: **donors pay** a commission.
- Stakeholder document: **donors earn** rewards.

**Fix:** decide one rule, for example: *donating surplus is free and earns stars, while paid orders and enterprise tools carry the fees.*

## Gap 7: Legal and safety items are missing

- **FSSAI licence** rules for sellers (shops, Gruh Udyog, cloud kitchens)
- **GST** on commission and delivery fees
- **Gig-worker status** for riders
- **Food-safety liability** for PG and hostel meals
- **Payment gateway** charges, usually about 2% of each payment

## Gap 8: Too many stakeholders at once

Eight groups is too many for a first launch.

**Fix:** pilot with **three: restaurants or hotels, NGOs, and riders.** Add PGs, hostels, shops and Gruh Udyog later.

## Missing revenue ideas you can add

- Paid **featured listings and business profiles**. The document offers "marketing support" for free, so charge for it.
- **Subscription** for hotels and cloud kitchens with extra tools.
- **Sponsor-a-meal** and **CSR reward-pool sponsorship**, as described above.
- **Impact reports**, which the first business model already has.

## Quick summary

1. Add a platform cut (commission) on paid orders.
2. Find who funds the stars and the rider pay.
3. Keep rescue and paid orders separate.
4. Choose one clear rule for who pays what.
5. Start with 3 stakeholders, not 8.
